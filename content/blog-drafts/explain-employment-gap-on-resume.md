A gap on your resume isn't the problem most people fear. An unexplained one that leaves a reader guessing causes more trouble than a clear one.

## On the resume

- **Use years only** when it's truthful and common in your field, but don't try to hide dates that a form will ask for anyway.
- **Add a one-line entry** for longer gaps, like "Career break: family care, 2024 to 2025."
- **Include what's relevant,** such as courses, freelance work or volunteering, without inflating it.

You don't owe an employer private medical or family details. "Personal health, now fully resolved" is enough, or even just "career break."

## In an interview

Use a three-part answer: reason, what you did, and why you're ready now. Keep it under 30 seconds.

**After a layoff:** "My role was cut when the company restructured. I took the time to finish a data certification, and I'm looking for an analyst role where I can use it."

**After caregiving:** "I took a year to care for a family member. That's now settled, and I'm ready to return full time."

**After study:** "I went back to complete a certificate in UX, and I built two projects during it, which I can walk you through."

Then stop talking and let them move on. Over-explaining makes a gap seem larger than it is.

## Common mistakes

- Changing dates to cover a gap: it can surface in a background check
- Sounding apologetic
- Criticising a former employer

If yours came from a layoff, also see [how to explain a layoff in an interview](/blog/explain-layoff-in-interview/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How long a gap is too long?

There's no fixed limit. Longer gaps may invite more questions, so prepare your answer and show what's current in your skills.

### Should I mention my gap in my cover letter?

Only if it helps, for instance if you're returning to the field after several years. One sentence is enough.
