A career change resume has one extra job: to answer "why is this person applying here?" before the reader has to ask.

## Open with where you're going

State your target at the top: "Operations manager moving into product management, with four years of working directly with engineering on internal tools." Without that line, a reader will see only your old title.

## Translate your experience

Old-industry language can hide relevant skills. Replace jargon with what the new field would call it.

**Teacher to corporate trainer:** "Taught 30 students" becomes "Designed and delivered a year-long training program for 30 learners, with assessments."

**Sales to customer success:** "Exceeded quota" stays, and you add "Managed renewals and onboarding for a set of accounts."

## Reorganize around relevance

You may use a "Relevant experience" section, listing your closest work first, with the rest under "Other experience." Keep your dates honest and clear.

## Prove you've committed

Recent evidence eases the risk an employer sees:

- A project you built in the new field
- A course with something to show for it
- Freelance or volunteer work
- Community involvement

One real project often outweighs a list of certificates.

## Lower your sights by one step, if needed

Applying at a level just below your current one may be sensible when switching fields. It's a judgment call that depends on your finances.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I use a functional resume?

Be careful. Skills-only formats can look evasive, and they sometimes parse badly. A hybrid with clear dates works better.

### Do I need a new degree?

Sometimes, in regulated fields. In many others, projects and experience matter more, so check what real postings ask for.
