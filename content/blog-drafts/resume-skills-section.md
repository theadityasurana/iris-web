A skills section helps recruiters scan and search. It can also hurt, if it's a dumping ground of everything you've touched.

## What to include

- **Tools and technologies** that you can actually use
- **Methods and domain skills:** financial modelling, usability testing, SEO
- **Languages,** with a realistic level
- **Certifications,** if relevant, or in their own section

## How to group them

Short, clear groups are easy to scan:

> **Languages:** Python, Go, SQL
> **Infrastructure:** Docker, Kubernetes, AWS
> **Data:** PostgreSQL, Redis, Kafka

Put the group most relevant to the target role first.

## What to leave out

- **Soft skills as bare words:** "communication" and "leadership" prove nothing here, so show them in your bullets
- **Universal tools:** Word and email, unless the role specifically needs them
- **Old or abandoned tools** you wouldn't want to use again
- **Anything you couldn't discuss in an interview**

## How many?

Enough to cover what's true and relevant, and few enough to read at a glance. If you need to reduce the list, cut the skills you least want to use in your next job. Your resume shapes what you get hired to do.

## Skill levels

Avoid star ratings and progress bars. They don't mean anything concrete and can parse badly. Rather than rating, show your depth through your achievements.

## Pinning your top skills

In your Iris profile, you can star your top skills, which are the ones that shape your matches and that recruiters see first. The rest stay on your profile. The same thinking applies anywhere: decide on your top few, then make them obvious.

For finding the right terms, see [resume keywords](/blog/resume-keywords-how-to-find-and-use-them/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Where should the skills section go?

Near the top for technical and career-change resumes, lower down if your experience already makes them obvious.

### Should I list skills I'm still learning?

Yes, if you label them honestly, for example "Learning: Rust." Don't list them as if you've mastered them.
