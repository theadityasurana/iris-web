Those short questions on application forms often do more filtering than your resume. Some are knockout questions, where one answer may end your application automatically.

## Common questions and how to approach them

### Are you legally authorized to work in this country?

Answer exactly and truthfully. If you're unsure about your status, check official guidance or ask an immigration professional. This isn't a place for guessing.

### Will you now or in the future require sponsorship?

Read the wording closely. If you're on a time-limited status, "in the future" may apply to you even if you don't need sponsorship today. See [how to find visa sponsorship jobs](/blog/how-to-find-visa-sponsorship-jobs/).

### How many years of experience do you have with X?

Count honestly, and be ready to explain it. Professional and project experience can be different, so if a free-text box exists, use it to clarify.

### What are your salary expectations?

Research the market for the role and location. If you must give a figure, give a range you would genuinely accept. See [how to answer salary questions](/blog/how-to-answer-salary-expectations/).

### When can you start?

Give your real notice period. An overstated availability causes problems later on.

## Keep answers consistent

An employer may notice if your form says five years and your resume implies three. Keep a short document of your standard answers so that each application matches.

## Handling a question you don't fit

If a role asks for five years and you have four, with closely relevant work, it's reasonable to apply and answer truthfully. Don't inflate the number. If the role requires a license or clearance you don't hold, skip it.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a knockout question?

A question whose answer may automatically disqualify you, such as one on required work authorization or a mandatory license.

### Can I leave salary blank?

If the field is optional, you can. Many forms require it, so have a researched range ready.
