Following up rarely hurts when it's short and polite. It helps most when your application might otherwise be buried.

## When to follow up

Wait about a week. If the posting gives a timeline, respect it. If you've already interviewed, the timing is different: send thanks within a day, then follow up after the date they gave you. See [what to do after an interview](/blog/thank-you-email-after-interview/).

## Who to contact

In order of preference:

1. Someone you've already spoken to at the company
2. The recruiter named on the posting or responsible for the team
3. The hiring manager for the role

Don't message several people at once with the same note, as they may compare.

## A template you can adapt

> Hi Priya, I applied for the Backend Engineer role last week. I've spent three years building payment APIs in Go, which looks close to what your team needs. If it's useful, I'm happy to share more. Thanks for your time.

It's short, specific, and asks for nothing that takes effort. Use your own details, and change it enough that it sounds like you.

## What to avoid

- "Just checking in" with no information
- Attaching your resume again without being asked
- Several messages in one week
- Asking why you haven't heard back

## When to stop

Send one follow-up, and at most one more later. After that, mark it closed in [your tracker](/blog/how-to-track-job-applications/) and put your energy elsewhere. A company that wants you can always reach you.

## Keeping replies in one place

Follow-ups only work if you see the answer. Iris gives each user a dedicated application inbox, so recruiter replies land in the app next to the job they relate to. You can reply from there.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it okay to follow up on LinkedIn?

Yes, a short direct message is common. Email is fine as well if you have a real address.

### Does following up annoy recruiters?

One polite message rarely does. Repeated messages might. Recruiters are busy, so silence usually isn't personal.
