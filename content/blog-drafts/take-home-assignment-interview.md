Take-home tasks can be a fair way to show what you can do, away from the pressure of a live test. They can also take over your week if you let them.

## Ask before you start

- How much time do you expect this to take?
- What are you looking for when you evaluate it?
- Can I use my usual tools, including AI assistants?
- When is it due, and is there flexibility?
- Will I get to discuss it afterward?

## Protect your time

Respect the stated time limit, roughly. If it says three hours, spending thirty won't necessarily impress, and it sets an expectation you can't keep up. If you have to go over, say what you did and why.

## What good submissions share

- **The brief is answered.** Read it twice, and do what it asks.
- **A short note explains your thinking:** the assumptions you made, the trade-offs, and what you'd do with more time.
- **It's easy to review:** clear instructions and tidy formatting.
- **It's yours.** You must be able to explain every part in a follow-up.

## When to push back

Some requests go beyond a fair assessment. Warning signs include work that looks like a real deliverable for the company, no clear limit on time, or a request for real strategy on their live product before any interview. You can politely offer an alternative: "I can't commit that much time at this stage, but I'd be glad to walk you through a similar project."

Declining may end that process. That's your call, and it depends on how much you want the job.

## After you submit

Note the date in [your tracker](/blog/how-to-track-job-applications/), and follow up if the promised date goes by. Assessment emails get their own category in Iris's Tracker, so a task with a deadline doesn't get lost.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use AI on a take-home?

Check the instructions, or ask. If allowed, be open about it, and make sure that you understand everything you submit.

### Should I ask for feedback?

Yes. Not everyone gives it, but asking is reasonable.
