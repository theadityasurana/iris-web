The top of your resume is where a reader decides whether to continue. Your summary should answer one question quickly: what do you do, and why should I keep reading?

## A simple formula

**Role + experience + proof + what's next.**

Two or three lines is enough.

## Examples

**Mid-career engineer:** Backend engineer with five years building payment systems in Go and PostgreSQL. Led the migration that moved our largest service off a legacy monolith. Looking for senior roles on platform teams.

**Career changer:** Former teacher moving into instructional design, with six years building curricula and a recent certificate in learning technology. Built three online courses used across my school.

**Fresher:** Computer science graduate from a state university with internship experience in React and two shipped projects, including a campus events app used by students. Seeking entry-level frontend roles.

These examples are illustrations, so use your own true numbers.

## Delete these

- Hard-working, results-driven, passionate team player
- Seeking a challenging position to utilise my skills
- Anything true of nearly everyone

## Make it specific to the role

Change one line for each type of role you target. It's the fastest form of tailoring and it's where a reader looks first. For deeper changes, see [how to tailor a resume without lying](/blog/tailor-resume-without-lying/).

## Reuse it

Application forms often ask for the same blurb. Iris stores your professional summary in your profile and uses it when a form asks about you, so write it once with care and check it now and then.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I use a summary or an objective?

Experienced candidates should use a summary. An early-career candidate can blend the two, with what they've done and what they want next.

### Should it be in the first person?

Resume convention drops the "I." Either is acceptable if you stay consistent.
