"Spray and pray" has a bad reputation, and some of it is earned. But the real problem is not how many applications you send. It is whether each one makes sense.

## When high volume backfires

Sending lots of applications causes trouble in a few specific ways:

- **Repeat applications at one company.** A recruiter who sees you apply to a dozen unrelated roles at the same company may reasonably conclude you did not read them.
- **Wrong answers on screening questions.** Automated tools that guess can state something untrue about your work authorization or experience. That is hard to walk back.
- **No learning.** If every application is untargeted, a lack of replies tells you nothing about what to change.
- **Interviews you can't speak to.** Getting a call about a job you don't remember applying to is a poor way to begin.

## When high volume is fine

Applying widely is reasonable when the roles genuinely match your skills and level, each company sees one relevant application, and the answers are accurate. Many job seekers need to apply broadly, particularly in a slow market or right after a layoff. There is nothing wrong with that.

## A better rule than "fewer applications"

Ask one question before each application: would I take this job if they offered it? If the answer is yes, apply. That single check removes most of the harm.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a company see that I applied to many of its jobs?

Usually yes. Most applicant tracking systems keep a candidate record, so a recruiter can often see your other applications at that company.

### Should I apply to several roles at the same company?

A few closely related roles is normal. If the roles have nothing in common, pick the best fit.
