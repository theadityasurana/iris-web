Being asked why you left is harder after a layoff, mostly because it's still raw. The answer itself can be simple. Interviewers hear it often.

## The structure

**What happened + brief context + what's next.**

## Scripts

**Company-wide cuts:** "The company cut about a third of its staff during a restructuring, and my team was affected. I'm proud of the billing project I led there, and I'm looking for a role where I can do more of that."

**Team closed:** "The company closed the product line I worked on, and the whole team was let go. It gave me a chance to think about what I want next, which is a role like this one."

**Only you were affected:** "My role was eliminated when the team's priorities changed. I learned a lot about working with enterprise clients, and I'd like to build on that here."

Change the details so it's true for you. Don't state numbers you aren't sure about.

## What to avoid

- Blaming your manager or leadership
- Long stories about what went wrong
- Sounding as though you're apologizing
- Pretending that you left by choice, since references or a background check may show otherwise

## Practise it out loud

Saying it ten times in private makes it boring, and boring is what you want. If it's still painful, a friend can help you practise until you can say it calmly.

## What if a layoff involved performance?

If you were let go for performance, be honest without dwelling on it: what you learned and what's different now. Don't call it a layoff if it wasn't.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I mention the layoff in my resume?

Generally not. Your dates show the end of the role, and you can explain when it comes up.

### Will being laid off count against me?

Layoffs are common and many employers understand them. How you talk about it and what you can do matter more.
