Very few resume rules are absolute, and this is one of those. Norms also differ by country and field.

## A practical guide

- **Students and early career:** one page is usually enough.
- **Mid-career:** one or two pages, depending on how much is relevant.
- **Senior and leadership:** two pages is common.
- **Academic and some government roles:** often longer formats, with their own conventions.

## What really matters

A reader will often form a view from the top third of page one. So your summary, latest role and best achievements must appear there. Page two only gets read if page one earns it.

## What to cut first

1. Roles from long ago, reduced to one line or dropped
2. Duties anyone in the role would have
3. Old tools and irrelevant courses
4. Your address beyond city and country
5. "References available on request"

## What not to do

Don't shrink your font and margins until it's hard to read. A tight, tiny page is worse than a clean page and a bit.

If you do go to two pages, make the second worth reading. A few lines on page two looks unfinished, so either trim down or fill it with relevant material.

## The test

Hand your resume to someone for ten seconds, then take it back. Ask what you do and what you're good at. If they can't say, the issue is your structure, not your length.

For what to write up top, see [how to write a resume summary](/blog/how-to-write-a-resume-summary/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a longer resume hurt in an ATS?

Software generally reads every page, so length mostly matters for human readers.

### Are CVs different?

In some countries "CV" just means resume. In academia, it often means a longer, complete record. Follow local norms.
